Modern digital systems rely heavily on cryptographic algorithms to preserve the authenticity and integrity of files. Standardised security mechanisms, such as Rivest-Shamir-Adleman (RSA) and the Elliptic Curve Digital Signature Algorithm (ECDSA), rely on integer factorization and discrete logarithms. However, these may soon become a thing of the past – the long-anticipated upcoming debut of reasonably powerful quantum computers poses a significant and inevitable risk to the privacy and integrity of the data protected by widely adopted classic public key cryptography. While current schemes are theoretically possible to solve, in practice, it would take an enormous amount of time, rendering such attempts useless. Although these computational problems are challenging to overcome using traditional computational methods, quantum attackers have proven that it is, in fact, possible to easily crack them in polynomial time using Shor's algorithm. However, despite the current lack of mature technologies capable of cracking such algorithms, the vulnerability poses a genuine danger in the foreseeable future. Adversaries employing the "harvest now, decrypt later" (HNDL) tactic are actively accumulating significant volumes of encrypted data today, intending to decrypt it later when the quantum computational infrastructure becomes sufficiently developed for such tasks. This is a critical and immediate strategic threat to all organisations that rely solely on signing and hashing methods, which are vulnerable to quantum attacks. Cloud environments are particularly vulnerable due to the frequent exchange of sensitive data across untrusted networks and their reliance on persistent key material.

As a result of this hazard, the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) initiated the Post-Quantum Cryptography (PQC) project to secure electronic information against future quantum threats. The standardisation of algorithms resistant to quantum attacks led to their initial approval for use in industry and government. In August 2024, NIST published a preliminary list of recommended algorithms: FIPS 203 (Module-Lattice Key-Encapsulation Mechanism, ML-KEM), FIPS 204 (Module-Lattice Digital Signature Algorithm, ML-DSA), and FIPS 205 (Stateless Hash-Based Digital Signature Algorithm, SLH-DSA). This was followed in October 2024 by FIPS 206 (FN-DSA-based Digital Signature Algorithm, FN-DSA), completing the initial set of standardised post-quantum signature schemes. This marked a significant step away from vulnerable cryptographic methods that are not protected against quantum computers. The urgency to adopt quantum-resistant strategies increased after the U.S. National Security Memorandum NSM-10 set deadlines for federal systems to fully transition to quantum-safe infrastructure. High-risk sectors like healthcare and finance should migrate critical infrastructure components within 2030-2035 to remain compliant with safety requirements ~\cite{nist-ir8547-2024,nist-nccoe-pqc-migration-2023}.

Integration of quantum-resistant schemes in the file-handling cloud workflows requires deep exploration with up-to-date, relevant knowledge of the cryptographic methods. To attempt to come closer to the optimal solution and address the key challenge, this dissertation conducts a benchmark for a matrix of combinations of different algorithms to determine the feasibility of a shift toward PQC technologies in established environments.

Problem Statement
Existing evaluations for the quantum-resistant algorithms are commonly conducted in isolation, measuring mere performance in its raw form. Even with the system-level benchmarking, there is a decent number of publications. However, a limited number of file-handling workflow studies have been made with cloud-like environments as their base. These evaluations typically omit the actual key aspects of real-world production deployments. Of particular interest to organisations utilising a cloud environment should be performance metrics such as interaction with storage systems, end-to-end request handling, verification workflows and integrity validation.

As a result, empirical understanding of the behaviour of quantum-resistant schemes is limited. Moreover, companies would need to understand what the benefits and tradeoffs are of relying solely on classical algorithms versus converting the entire architecture to handle PQC only. As system pipelines have limited bandwidth and associated cost, the most balanced solution needs to be employed as a result.
Furthermore, there are active discussions suggesting employing hybrid approaches – where classical and post-quantum mechanisms are combined and meant to serve as a transitioning solution while novel technologies are still undergoing field testing. Yet, the practicality of such a technique remains insufficiently explored to derive meaningful recommendations.

Project aim and objectives
The aim of this project is to implement a prototype of a security-hardened, backend-centric system for demonstrating sample usage workflows for signature and verification using various hashing and signature techniques. This prototype will also be used for exploring the efficiency and feasibility of the PQC-only and hybrid approach against classical. While there will be a web user interface for demonstration purposes, the actual benchmarking of this system will be conducted via a Command-Line Interface (CLI) to prevent any performance penalties associated with browser operations. For each assessment campaign, the tests are going to be conducted in a controlled and reproducible randomised sequence of file-processing workflow runs.

To achieve these results, this project sets a list of objectives.

1. Design a system architecture that enforces consistent cryptographic execution and minimises client-side variability.

2. Implement support for a variety of classical, post-quantum, and hybrid signature and hash profiles over a shared data model. Selected algorithms span classical schemes, NIST-standardised post-quantum candidates, and pre-standardisation schemes, in order to provide a representative coverage of the current cryptographic landscape.

3. Develop a structure for canonical manifest generation to guarantee deterministic and reproducible outputs.

4. Integrate mechanisms for secure file hashing, signing, storage, and verification. For the verification flow, develop a framework for checking file integrity beyond mere hash matching.

5. Construct a benchmarking framework that generates reproducible performance measurements across multiple configurations. Randomise the runs with configurable seeds for deterministic measurements sequence.

A combination of these objectives integrated into a single prototype will enable comprehensive performance assessment of system-level file handling in cloud-based workflows.

Research Questions

This dissertation is guided by the following research questions:

RQ1: What is the performance overhead of post-quantum and hybrid signature schemes compared to classical signatures in a complete end-to-end file-process lifecycle?

RQ2: How does hybrid signing affect latency and system throughput at matching bandwidth relative to classical-only and PQC-only strategies?

RQ3: What architectural harnesses are required to enable reproducible results and analysis of cryptographic signature schemes?

RQ4: What are the security implications and trade-offs of integrating PQC and hybrid signatures into existing backend architecture?

2. LITERATURE REVIEW
================================================================================

2.1 Foundations of Classical Public-Key Cryptography
-----------------------------------------------------

The modern foundations of public-key cryptography were established by Diffie and
Hellman~\cite{diffie1976}, who introduced the concept of asymmetric key pairs and
a key exchange protocol grounded in the intractability of the discrete logarithm
problem. Shortly thereafter, Rivest, Shamir, and Adleman~\cite{rivest1978} proposed
the RSA cryptosystem, deriving its security from the computational hardness of
factoring large composite integers -- a problem that remains intractable for
classical computers but, as later demonstrated, is efficiently solvable by a
sufficiently powerful quantum computer.

Elliptic Curve Cryptography (ECC) emerged as a more efficient alternative to RSA,
independently proposed by Koblitz~\cite{koblitz1987} and Miller~\cite{miller1985}.
ECC achieves equivalent security levels with considerably smaller key sizes,
reducing computational and bandwidth overheads at comparable security strengths.
The Elliptic Curve Digital Signature Algorithm (ECDSA), standardised by NIST in
FIPS 186~\cite{nist-fips186-5}, is widely deployed across TLS, code signing, and
software supply chain integrity protocols. The EdDSA family, and in particular
Ed25519, was introduced by Bernstein et al.~\cite{bernstein2012eddsa}, offering
improved performance and stronger resistance to implementation-level side-channel
vulnerabilities compared to ECDSA, owing in part to its use of deterministic
nonce generation.

Symmetric authentication primitives such as HMAC were formalised by Bellare,
Canetti, and Krawczyk~\cite{bellare1996hmac}, providing a provably secure message
authentication code construction built on iterated hash functions. HMAC is widely
deployed in API authentication and session integrity workflows in cloud-facing
systems, and represents the most computationally lightweight of the signing
profiles evaluated in this work.

Cryptographic hash functions underpin the integrity guarantees of virtually all
digital signature schemes and file verification workflows. The SHA-2 family,
standardised in FIPS 180-4~\cite{nist-fips180-4}, remains the dominant choice in
production systems. SHA-3 (Keccak), standardised as FIPS 202~\cite{nist-fips202},
offers a structurally distinct design based on a sponge construction, providing
independence from the Merkle-Damgard framework used by SHA-2 and consequently a
different security profile. BLAKE3~\cite{blake3-2021} has demonstrated
substantially higher throughput than both SHA-2 and SHA-3 on modern hardware,
making it a compelling option for high-volume file hashing pipelines; it has not
yet undergone formal NIST standardisation but has seen significant adoption in
performance-sensitive systems.

2.2 The Quantum Computing Threat to Classical Cryptography
----------------------------------------------------------

The security of RSA, ECDSA, and Diffie-Hellman key exchange rests on the
computational intractability of integer factorisation and the discrete logarithm
problem respectively. Shor~\cite{shor1994} demonstrated that a sufficiently
powerful quantum computer can solve both problems in polynomial time, fundamentally
invalidating the security assumptions underlying all widely deployed public-key
cryptosystems. The consequences extend to elliptic-curve variants: the elliptic
curve discrete logarithm problem is equally susceptible to Shor's algorithm,
rendering ECDSA and EdDSA insecure against quantum adversaries~\cite{bernstein2017}.

Grover's algorithm~\cite{grover1996} further showed that quantum search provides a
quadratic speedup against symmetric primitives and hash functions, effectively
halving their security parameter. While this is less catastrophic than the
exponential speedup Shor provides against public-key cryptography, it implies
that hash functions used for file integrity must offer at least twice the classical
bit-security to retain equivalent protection against quantum search. SHA-256, for
example, drops from 128-bit to 64-bit security under Grover, which has informed
NIST's recommendation to transition to 256-bit symmetric keys and larger hash
outputs for long-term security.

Bernstein and Lange~\cite{bernstein2017} provided a comprehensive review of the
post-quantum landscape, surveying the major families of quantum-resistant candidate
algorithms and the cryptographic hardness assumptions underlying them. Their
analysis highlighted that transitioning from classical to post-quantum infrastructure
represents not merely an algorithmic substitution but a rearchitecting of
public-key cryptography across all digital systems.

Mosca~\cite{mosca2018} introduced a probabilistic risk model arguing that the
quantum threat should be evaluated by the intersection of: the expected time until
cryptographically relevant quantum computers become available; the time required
to migrate existing cryptographic infrastructure; and the required security shelf
life of the protected data. Where the sum of migration time and computation time
exceeds the data's shelf life, an organisation is already in a state of
cryptographic risk regardless of whether a quantum computer currently exists.

The engineering timeline for fault-tolerant quantum computers capable of running
Shor's algorithm at cryptographically relevant scales remains a subject of active
research. Preskill~\cite{preskill2018} characterised the near-term landscape as
the "Noisy Intermediate-Scale Quantum" (NISQ) era, in which devices are too small
and error-prone to threaten current public-key parameters. However, the rapid pace
of hardware development has prompted institutions including NIST, NSA, and the
BSI to treat the threat as a concrete planning horizon rather than a speculative
future risk~\cite{nsm10-2022,bsi-pqc-2021}.

2.3 Harvest Now, Decrypt Later and Long-Term Data Exposure
----------------------------------------------------------

The HNDL threat model is a particularly acute concern for long-lived sensitive
data. Adversaries can record ciphertext today and defer decryption until
sufficient quantum computational capacity is available. Unlike conventional
attacks whose impact is bounded to the moment of breach, HNDL extends the
effective attack horizon over the entire future lifetime of quantum hardware
development. This threat was formally recognised in the U.S. National Security
Memorandum NSM-10~\cite{nsm10-2022}, which directed federal agencies to inventory
cryptographic assets and initiate migration to quantum-safe alternatives.

Mosca~\cite{mosca2018} formalised the urgency of this threat: organisations
handling data whose confidentiality must be preserved beyond the likely quantum
threshold -- classified intelligence, medical records, financial transaction
histories, or long-lived signing keys -- face an immediate strategic risk even in
the absence of a current quantum adversary. This is particularly relevant in cloud
environments, where large volumes of encrypted data are persistently stored in
object storage and transmitted across infrastructure boundaries that may be
subject to passive interception.

The implications for file-signing systems are direct: signing keys that are
long-lived and cryptographically weak against quantum adversaries may be exposed
through HNDL attacks, enabling an adversary to retroactively forge signatures
on historical manifests. This motivates the transition to signing schemes whose
security does not rest on problems tractable by Shor's algorithm.

2.4 The NIST Post-Quantum Cryptography Standardisation Programme
----------------------------------------------------------------

In response to the quantum threat, NIST launched a multi-year open competition
to standardise quantum-resistant cryptographic algorithms. The programme was
initiated with the publication of NIST Internal Report 8105~\cite{nistir8105-2016},
which documented the rationale and submission requirements. Following three rounds
of public evaluation, NIST published its third-round status report in NIST IR
8413~\cite{nistir8413-2022}, identifying four algorithms for standardisation and
a set of alternates for further study.

In August 2024, NIST published three Federal Information Processing Standards:
FIPS 203~\cite{nist-fips203}, specifying the Module-Lattice-Based Key-Encapsulation
Mechanism (ML-KEM); FIPS 204~\cite{nist-fips204}, specifying the Module-Lattice
Digital Signature Algorithm (ML-DSA); and FIPS 205~\cite{nist-fips205}, specifying
the Stateless Hash-Based Digital Signature Algorithm (SLH-DSA). In October 2024,
FIPS 206~\cite{nist-fips206} was published, standardising the FN-DSA-based Digital
Signature Algorithm (FN-DSA). NIST IR 8547~\cite{nist-ir8547-2024} subsequently
provided transition guidance, specifying deprecation timelines for classical
algorithms and recommended adoption pathways for government and critical
infrastructure. The associated NCCoE migration guide~\cite{nist-nccoe-pqc-migration-2023}
provides implementation-level guidance for organisations undertaking PQC migration.

This standardisation effort represents the most significant change to public-key
cryptographic practice since the widespread adoption of RSA and ECC in the 1990s,
and its implications for deployed systems -- including cloud-based file-handling
and signing infrastructure -- are the central motivation for the present work.

2.5 Lattice-Based Cryptography: CRYSTALS-ml_dsa and ML-DSA
-------------------------------------------------------------

Lattice-based cryptography has emerged as the dominant paradigm for post-quantum
signature schemes, owing to strong security reductions, relatively compact key
and signature sizes, and efficient software implementations. The security of
lattice-based schemes rests primarily on the Learning With Errors (LWE) problem,
introduced by Regev~\cite{regev2005}, which is conjectured to be hard for both
classical and quantum adversaries. The Module-LWE variant, which operates over
polynomial quotient rings to reduce key sizes, underpins both ML-KEM and ML-DSA.

Lyubashevsky~\cite{lyubashevsky2012} introduced the Fiat-Shamir with Aborts
framework, which provides a practical construction for lattice-based signatures
without requiring a trapdoor sampler. CRYSTALS-ml_dsa~\cite{ducas2018}, built
upon this approach, was submitted to the NIST competition and extensively analysed
across its parameter sets. The ml_dsa parameter set -- targeting NIST security
category 3 (comparable to AES-192) -- has been widely studied in both theoretical
and empirical work, and its performance serves as a baseline for assessing the
practical cost of PQC signature adoption.

The standardised form, ML-DSA (FIPS 204~\cite{nist-fips204}), retains the core
design of CRYSTALS-ml_dsa with minor specification changes to satisfy NIST's
requirements for determinism and algorithmic agility. As both the pre-standardisation
ml_dsa and the standardised ML-DSA remain in active deployment -- often in
parallel within migration-period systems -- their comparative performance at the
system level, rather than in isolated microbenchmarks, is an open empirical
question.

2.6 Hash-Based Signatures: SPHINCS+ and SLH-DSA
------------------------------------------------

Hash-based signature schemes offer a fundamentally conservative security model:
their security relies exclusively on the collision resistance and preimage
resistance of the underlying hash function, with no dependence on algebraic
hardness assumptions. This makes them among the most defensively credible PQC
candidates, as their security degrades gracefully under Grover's algorithm rather
than collapsing catastrophically as RSA and ECDSA do under Shor's.

Early hash-based constructions include the one-time signature scheme of
Lamport~\cite{lamport1979} and the Merkle tree-based multi-signature extension of
Merkle~\cite{merkle1989}. Stateful schemes such as XMSS~\cite{buchmann2011} and
LMS extended this foundation to support many signing operations at the cost of
state management -- a significant operational burden in distributed systems.
Bernstein et al.~\cite{bernstein2015sphincs} proposed SPHINCS, the first practical
stateless hash-based signature scheme, eliminating state management requirements
at the cost of larger signature sizes. SPHINCS+~\cite{bernstein2019sphincsplus}
refined this design through the introduction of FORS (Forest of Random Subsets),
achieving improved security proofs and better parameter trade-offs. SLH-DSA (FIPS
205~\cite{nist-fips205}) standardises SPHINCS+ for interoperability.

Hash-based schemes produce substantially larger signatures than lattice-based
alternatives -- SLH-DSA signatures range from roughly 8 KB to 50 KB depending on
the parameter set -- which introduces non-trivial payload overheads in file-handling
pipelines, signed manifests, and API responses. Understanding the concrete
throughput and latency impact of this overhead in a microservice signing workflow,
rather than in the abstract, is one of the motivating questions for this dissertation.

2.7 NTRU-Based Signatures: FN-DSA and FN-DSA
--------------------------------------------

FN-DSA~\cite{fouque2020} is a lattice-based signature scheme constructed over
NTRU lattices, a family first described by Hoffstein, Pipher, and
Silverman~\cite{hoffstein1998}. Unlike ml_dsa, which is based on rejection
sampling, FN-DSA uses a Gaussian trapdoor sampler over NTRU lattices to produce
signatures, achieving very compact output: FN-DSA-512 produces signatures of
approximately 666 bytes, compared to approximately 2.4 KB for ml_dsa/ML-DSA.

This compactness comes at the cost of implementation complexity: the Fast Fourier
Transform-based Gaussian sampler is numerically sensitive and requires careful
floating-point handling to achieve constant-time operation, making FN-DSA
considerably more difficult to implement securely than ml_dsa. Fouque et al.
provided reference implementations and security proofs; NIST standardised FN-DSA
as FN-DSA in FIPS 206~\cite{nist-fips206}.

The performance profile of FN-DSA differs meaningfully from that of ML-DSA: key
generation and signing are substantially slower due to the Gaussian sampler, but
verification is fast and signature sizes are roughly one-third those of ml_dsa.
In contexts where bandwidth is a more significant constraint than computation --
such as object storage pipelines or high-throughput API gateways -- FN-DSA's
smaller signatures may offer a practical advantage that is invisible in isolated
algorithm benchmarks.

2.8 Hybrid Cryptographic Approaches
------------------------------------

A hybrid signature scheme simultaneously applies a classical and a post-quantum
algorithm, such that a valid signature requires correctness under both. The
security guarantee is that the scheme remains secure provided at least one
component is unbroken -- offering protection against the (unlikely but
non-zero) possibility that a newly published cryptanalytic result breaks a PQC
algorithm before migration is complete, while simultaneously protecting against
a quantum adversary that breaks the classical component~\cite{bindel2019}.

Bindel et al.~\cite{bindel2019} provided a composability framework for hybrid
key encapsulation and authenticated key exchange, demonstrating that combining
independent schemes preserves security under standard assumptions. The European
Telecommunications Standards Institute~\cite{etsi-pqc-2020} and the German
Federal Office for Information Security (BSI)~\cite{bsi-pqc-2021} have both
issued technical guidelines recommending hybrid schemes as a transitional measure
for high-assurance environments during the PQC migration period. The IETF has
published drafts specifying hybrid constructions for TLS and X.509, reflecting
growing consensus around hybrid as an intermediate deployment strategy.

From an engineering perspective, hybrid schemes introduce additive overhead: a
signed manifest under an RSA-PSS + ml_dsa hybrid, for instance, must carry
both sets of key material and signatures, increasing payload size and signing
latency compared to either scheme alone. The practical significance of this
overhead in a microservice architecture depends on the characteristics of the
underlying workflow -- particularly the relative cost of network I/O, storage
operations, and cryptographic computation. Empirically measuring these trade-offs
under controlled conditions is necessary to provide actionable guidance to
organisations evaluating hybrid deployment strategies.

2.9 Performance Benchmarking of Post-Quantum Signature Schemes
--------------------------------------------------------------

A substantial body of literature exists on the isolated computational performance
of PQC algorithms. The eBACS (ECRYPT Benchmarking of Cryptographic Systems)
project~\cite{bernstein-ebacs} provides publicly available cycle counts for a
wide range of cryptographic implementations across hardware platforms, establishing
a standard reference for algorithm-level performance comparison. The pqm4
project~\cite{kannwischer2019} extended this to embedded ARM Cortex-M4
microcontrollers, demonstrating which NIST candidates are viable for
resource-constrained deployment.

At the network and systems level, Paquin, Stebila, and Tamvada~\cite{paquin2020}
evaluated PQC algorithms integrated into TLS 1.3, measuring handshake latency
and connection overhead under realistic network conditions. Their findings
highlighted that signature sizes and key transmission costs have a more pronounced
impact on system-level throughput than raw cryptographic operation speed,
particularly under bandwidth-constrained conditions. Sikeridis et al.~\cite{sikeridis2020}
conducted a complementary study examining the impact of PQC certificate chains on
TLS 1.3 connection establishment across varied network conditions, finding that
the larger key and signature material of PQC schemes significantly increased
handshake latency in high-latency network environments.

Despite this body of work, system-level benchmarking in the specific context of
file-handling, manifest generation, and multi-step integrity verification workflows
remains underexplored. Existing studies predominantly focus on TLS handshakes or
isolated microbenchmarks, and few address the compound latency of complete
file-processing lifecycles -- covering upload, hash computation, signed manifest
generation, object storage interaction, and cryptographic verification -- in a
microservice architecture representative of a production cloud environment. The
present work aims to address this gap by benchmarking a full end-to-end signing
and verification pipeline across a matrix of algorithm and hash combinations.

2.10 Cloud Security, Microservices, and Cryptographic Integration
-----------------------------------------------------------------

Cloud computing environments introduce security considerations that go beyond
those of conventional on-premises infrastructure. Subashini and
Kavitha~\cite{subashini2011} surveyed security challenges across cloud service
delivery models, identifying data integrity, key management, and the trust boundary
between tenants and providers as primary concerns. Zissis and Lekkas~\cite{zissis2012}
examined how public-key infrastructure and digital signatures can be adapted to
meet the integrity and authenticity requirements of cloud-hosted workloads,
concluding that certificate management and revocation present significant
operational challenges in dynamic cloud environments.

The microservice architectural pattern~\cite{dragoni2017}, now prevalent in
cloud-native application design, introduces additional complexity for cryptographic
integration: each service boundary constitutes a potential attack surface, and
the distribution of signing keys across service components requires granular
access control. In such environments, the performance impact of cryptographic
operations is compounded by inter-service communication latency, making
end-to-end benchmarking particularly valuable for understanding realistic system
behaviour as opposed to the performance of cryptographic primitives in isolation.

2.11 File Integrity Verification and Signed Manifests
-----------------------------------------------------

Canonical signed manifests -- documents that enumerate files alongside their
cryptographic hashes and associated metadata in a deterministic, serialisation-stable
form -- provide a foundation for auditable integrity verification that extends
beyond simple hash matching. By signing the manifest itself, rather than each
file independently, a system can guarantee the integrity and provenance of an
entire collection of files in a single verifiable artifact, while supporting
replay detection and version control at the manifest level.

The Update Framework (TUF)~\cite{cappos2008}, developed for securing software
update systems against a broad set of supply chain attacks, demonstrates how
layered signing and metadata structures can provide strong authenticity guarantees
even in the presence of partially compromised infrastructure. More recent
initiatives such as sigstore~\cite{newman2022sigstore} have generalised these
principles for cloud-native container and artifact signing, making manifest-based
integrity verification increasingly central to cloud security practice.

Transitioning manifest-based systems to PQC signatures requires schema evolution
to accommodate the larger key material and signatures of post-quantum schemes, as
well as canonical serialisation guarantees to ensure that deterministic
verification remains possible across implementations. These engineering concerns
are directly relevant to the prototype developed in this dissertation.

2.12 Reproducibility in Cryptographic Benchmarking
---------------------------------------------------

A persistent methodological challenge in cryptographic benchmarking is the
reproducibility of timing results. Noise from OS scheduling, CPU frequency scaling,
memory access patterns, and concurrent system processes can substantially distort
measurements, particularly for fast operations whose individual execution time is
on the order of microseconds. Standard approaches to mitigate this include
repeated measurement with statistical aggregation, warm-up runs to prime CPU
caches, and process isolation~\cite{bernstein-ebacs}.

Randomised ordering of benchmark conditions -- using configurable seeds to
generate a deterministic but varied execution sequence -- controls for order
effects and prevents systematic bias introduced by execution ordering, analogous
to randomised controlled trial methodology in empirical sciences. This approach
ensures that results are not artefacts of a particular execution sequence and
supports statistical comparison across algorithm conditions. Ensuring full
reproducibility of benchmark campaigns -- from dataset generation through to
statistical summary -- is a prerequisite for drawing valid conclusions about
relative algorithm performance and for enabling independent replication of results.

2.13 Research Gaps
------------------

The foregoing survey identifies several gaps that directly motivate the present
dissertation. First, while isolated PQC algorithm benchmarks are numerous, system-
level studies of post-quantum signature schemes in file-handling workflows --
encompassing hash computation, signed manifest generation, object storage
interaction, and multi-step verification -- are limited. Most published evaluations
do not capture the compound latency of a complete file-processing lifecycle in a
cloud-like environment. Second, empirical comparisons of classical-only, PQC-only,
and hybrid signing strategies within a unified, controlled microservice architecture
have not been published, making it difficult for organisations to assess the real
cost of hybrid deployment relative to a pure-PQC migration. Third, the specific
performance relationship between pre-standardisation schemes (CRYSTALS-ml_dsa)
and their NIST-standardised counterparts (ML-DSA) at the system level -- rather
than in cryptographic libraries -- is not documented. Fourth, the architectural
harnesses required to achieve reproducible, statistically valid benchmarking of
end-to-end cryptographic pipelines are insufficiently described in the existing
literature.

This dissertation addresses these gaps through the design and implementation of a
microservice-based prototype and an associated benchmarking framework that
systematically evaluates multiple signature and hash algorithm combinations under
controlled, reproducible conditions, providing empirical evidence to inform
organisational decisions about PQC migration strategy.
